AI can radically change how work gets done while still fitting familiar patterns of adaptation under pressure. But it can also influence what people accept as evidence and how intent becomes action. That changes the mechanisms we use to understand and control the system.

The framework can remain useful while our assumptions need rechecking. Decades of research give us a lens; they don’t guarantee that familiar review processes or feedback loops will remain adequate.

Economic Boundary

External conditions change what the business can sustain.

More room to operate

  • 📈Stronger demand and better pricing power
  • 💰Fresh funding or cheaper financing
  • 🏷️Lower energy, supplier or infrastructure costs

Less room to operate

  • 📉Market slowdown, churn or price competition
  • 🏦Costlier credit or funding that dries up
  • 💱Higher supplier costs or currency shocks

Sustainable capacity changes the boundary.
A tighter internal budget changes the target.

These are common external conditions, not measurements of a universal boundary. Holding the work and commitments broadly constant, stronger sustainable revenue, accessible capital or a lower sustainable cost base can expand financial viability. Losing them can contract it. Favorable demand also brings work: growth does not automatically increase every margin. An internal cost target or budget cut is a management decision; it is not itself the actual economic-failure constraint. Watch actual cash, obligations and value.

Activity, capacity and control

More consumption or commitments can move the operating state toward economic failure when costs outpace value and supporting financial capacity. Sustainable savings can expand viable operating space; reduced revenue, funding or runway can contract it. Forecasts provide feedback, and budgets or quotas are management targets and decision thresholds. Revised targets do not by themselves change the actual viability constraint. Rasmussen draws the perceived boundary and error margin only on the performance side.

Workload Boundary

The sustainable capacity to do and understand the work can change.

More room to operate

  • 👥More trained people and relevant expertise
  • 🛠️Better tools and reliable toil reduction
  • 🧘Protected focus, recovery time and knowledge sharing

Less room to operate

  • 🚪Attrition, layoffs or unavailable specialists
  • 🥵Fatigue, burnout and constant interruptions
  • 🧩Fragile tooling and lost operational knowledge

Less effective capacity shortens the playing surface.
More incoming work also moves the operating point.

Holding the kind and quality of required work broadly constant, usable expert capacity can change the sustainable workload limit. Headcount is an imperfect proxy: new hires need onboarding, relevant expertise, tools and coordination. Greater raw demand is not itself a capacity expansion; it can instead move the operating point toward the workload constraint. Automation can remove toil but adds maintenance and exception work. Burnout and lost knowledge can reduce future effective capacity. These examples are mechanisms for discussion, not a numerical mapping from staff count to diagram geometry.

Focused verification capacity

Effective capacity means relevant expertise, focused time and enough context for this work, not merely assigned headcount. A reviewer can be allocated to many tasks yet lack usable time to assess this change. Protect contextual knowledge and recovery; changing demand or removing expert capacity can also change the verification effort required per item.

Boundary of
Acceptable Performance

Crossing the functional limit means unacceptable outcomes.

Failure in the service

  • 🚨Outages and cascading system failures
  • 🔓Lost, corrupted or exposed customer data
  • ⏳Recovery that cannot restore service in time

Harm beyond the service

  • 🚧Customer operations disrupted; safety put at risk
  • 💔Trust and reputation damaged
  • ⚖️Contractual losses or regulatory consequences

Test people, tools and workflow together.
Can we use good output, catch mistakes and recover?

The acceptable-performance boundary is defined by the system's required functions and tolerated harms. Not every crossing is an immediate catastrophe: an unacceptable outcome can be local or escalate through dependencies. Availability, data integrity, confidentiality and safe recovery are software examples. Reputation, commercial losses and regulatory consequences can follow that failure; they are not separate Rasmussen boundaries. Formal SLOs and compliance checks are useful proxies, not an exact picture of the true functional limit. Crossing the dotted perceived boundary enters the protected error margin; it does not prove a failure has already occurred. The model does not provide a measurable distance to catastrophe.

Joint performance over the lifecycle

Evaluate people, tools and workflow together across good, mediocre and poor AI output. A model benchmark alone cannot establish operational joint performance. Chapter 5 §5.5.3 (printed pp. 85–86; PDF pp. 93–94) calls for evaluation situated in the actual work and repeated through the lifecycle.

Compare verified outcomes, revision and checking effort with a relevant baseline. Include a plausible consequential mistake in a controlled exercise: can people access the evidence, detect it and correct it before enactment? Measure outcome quality, verification effort, consequential-error detection, response time and successful recovery. Re-evaluate when models, workflow, staffing or dependencies materially change. These are proposed engineering measures rather than a universal score; operational signals remain imperfect proxies for the true functional limit.

Morey, Rayo and Woods (2025) studied predictive clinical AI in simulated patient cases with 450 nursing students and 12 licensed nurses. Recommendation correctness improved or degraded joint performance. Added explanations did not significantly change those effects in this experiment. It motivates the evaluation design; it is not an LLM software-risk estimate or evidence that explanations never help.

The dotted line represents a fallible perceived limit. Crossing it consumes the deliberately protected error margin; failure is not inevitable at that point. An alert does not push the operating point back. It needs timely interpretation, authority to slow or change work and people with tools to execute a response. Capacity, efficiency and redundancy may improve feasible performance, but need realistic load, failure and recovery testing: correlated dependencies can defeat nominal redundancy. Canaries, least privilege, independent review, rollback and restore exercises preserve bounded excursions and recovery. An error budget is a service-management mechanism, not a geometric measurement of Rasmussen's error margin.

Notice, intervene and recover

Deliberate interventions pause or redirect work to preserve margin. Someone needs the evidence, authority and capacity to act.

Notice requires usable signals and timely interpretation. Intervention requires evidence, authority, tools and remaining response capacity. Recovery requires contextual knowledge and fallbacks that people can use under pressure. A written rollback procedure does not establish that capability. Awareness and the margin itself exert no automatic restoring force; the objective is sufficient margin, bounded excursions and recoverability, not a preferred geometric center.

Exercise stale observations, scarce time, excess review demand and unavailable supporting services outside production. Assess whether the team can pause, reduce scope and recover. AI in the feedback and action channels

AI is not automatically a new controller. It influences channels used by existing controllers. On the feedback side it can gather and summarize observations, find contradictions and support testing; fluent summaries can also conceal uncertainty. On the action side it can present options, draft decisions and translate intent into output; plausible detail can exceed the communicator’s intended criteria.

AI output changes the operating state when people or automation admit and enact the resulting decisions, not merely when output is generated. The risk is conditional: AI can strengthen testing, diagnosis, challenge and verification. Risk rises when generation and action become easier faster than assurance and correction.

ETR · Aug 2026 · Uber CTO · Apr 2026 · Workday CEO / SEC · Feb 2025

ETR surveyed 1,636 technology leaders, June 2–July 5, 2026. Its 47% is reported spending above plan, not a prevalence estimate of annual budgets exhausted early. Among respondents reallocating to cover overruns, 61% cut contractor/consultant spending, 49% legacy infrastructure modernization or hardware refresh, 36% non-AI licenses and 28% internal headcount budgets. The subgroup denominator matters; a smaller headcount budget does not itself prove layoffs. The Uber case is an executive's reported account rather than audited finances. Workday's February 2025 memo announced roughly 1,750 eliminations, alongside continued strategic hiring and AI/platform priorities. It does not say every eliminated role was replaced by AI or every saved dollar funded AI. Other restructuring causes were also described. The portfolio-return example is an illustrative management choice. These cases support financial pressure and reallocation, not the claim that AI-related layoffs are universal or necessarily harmful. Cost control can preserve other margins by dropping low-value work. It transfers pressure when required output stays constant but relevant expertise, maintenance or assurance disappears. More AI expenditure moves the operating state; budgets remain targets, not an extra dotted economic boundary.

Whole-workflow economics

Include model use, platform commitments, integration, tools and data, evaluation, human verification, rework and recovery in outcome cost. Longer contexts, retries, agent loops and additional workflows can consume unit-cost savings. This is conditional, not a prediction that cheaper AI must increase spending. Evaluate work transferred or deferred to reviewers, maintainers and responders, along with future coupling, congestion and recovery costs.

Preserve capacity by reducing low-value demand, bounding loops, changing scope and routing or caching where verified quality permits. Organizations may also redirect portfolios toward products with stronger expected returns, as the visible example suggests. Keep verification and recovery funded. AI can improve tests and diagnosis as well as generation. If required output stays constant while relevant expertise or review is removed, workload can rise and performance margin can shrink.

Evidence, capacity and understanding

Least effort describes normal adaptation under competing goals and limited attention, not a moral claim about laziness. Plausible ≠ measured. Fluent ≠ intended. Fast generation does not establish fast verification, integration or maintenance. Conflicting AI proposals still require people to reconstruct context and understand fundamentals. The meat-proxy scenario is this talk’s illustration, not a claim that all AI use reduces expertise. Chapter 5 §§5.3.1–5.3.4 (printed pp. 82–83; PDF pp. 90–91) discusses filtering, revision and plausible errors that need expertise to recognize and correct.

Current LLM evidence shows changes in task pace and cognitive effort that differ by task and experience. Lee et al. (CHI 2025) surveyed 319 knowledge workers: critical-thinking effort shifted toward verification, response integration and task stewardship, while higher confidence in AI was associated with less scrutiny. This is self-reported association, not causal proof. Dell’Acqua et al. randomized 758 consultants: work was faster and higher quality within the model’s capability frontier, but correctness was lower outside it. These are task-specific results, not a universal effect or a whole-system risk estimate. Dillon et al. and Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond provide additional heterogeneous workplace evidence.

Preserve focused attention, contextual knowledge and usable authority to change commitments. Change scope, timing or admitted demand when checking cannot keep pace. AI can improve assurance capacity where joint evidence supports its testing and diagnosis. Demand relative to capacity can increase backlog and delay; defects additionally depend on adaptations that shed assurance. Do not treat generated backlog clearance as verified completion.

Close the loop

A persuasive explanation is not independent evidence. Enacted decisions can consume margin before the control structure observes the change. Rasmussen’s measuring-channel warning applies here: “No control system will perform better than its measuring channel.” Usable signals still need interpretation, practical authority and response capacity.

Retain the means to notice, challenge, stop and recover. Verify intent and source evidence; update monitoring probes, alerts and runbooks when service behaviour changes. Limit admitted work when assurance cannot keep pace. Use controlled exercises to evaluate useful output, convincing mistakes and recovery under pressure, and repeat evaluation after material changes. Monitoring proxies do not reveal the exact functional limit.

Admission creates a commitment

In the retry example, many agents can propose changes across many services. The useful question is how much work we choose to admit to the team's verification queue. Count work in progress and the age of outstanding reviews alongside complexity, checking effort and effective expert capacity. Headcount or raw change count alone misses differences in context and risk.

Agree a local admission limit and review-age trigger before increasing parallel work. Use the team's baseline and the consequences of delay to choose them, then revise them with experience. There is no universally correct ratio or ticket limit. Keep room for interrupts and variable work rather than planning every reviewer's attention to full utilization.

When the trigger fires, the named lead can pause new agent submissions, defer low-value changes or reduce scope. Management must accept the changed delivery expectation and protect time for reviewers. Keep urgent reliability or security work on a deliberate triage path. Pausing new work does not abandon work already owed.

Understanding is part of capacity

Give engineers time to explain the retry policy, inspect the dependency and practise diagnosis. AI can help comprehension and test discovery, provided someone checks that the evidence is useful. Validate any claimed reduction in review effort. Do not treat reviewer availability after hours as sustainable capacity.

Queue growth shows delay and capacity pressure. It does not by itself establish a defect increase. Performance risk additionally depends on adaptations such as bypassed checks or reduced testing. Add a short handoff to the existing change process rather than creating a parallel bureaucracy. State the intended behaviour, link the relevant requirement or dependency documentation, show what was tested, and identify uncertainty. For this retry change, inspect which errors are transient, whether the operation is safe to repeat, how attempts and elapsed time are bounded, and what happens when many callers retry together. AI as an assurance aid

Use AI to suggest edge cases, locate source evidence or draft tests. Validate the source and expected result independently against the requirement, a reference implementation or observed behaviour. A test copied from the same mistaken assumption can agree with faulty code. An additional model or a fluent explanation does not by itself provide independent evidence.

Choose checking depth according to the consequence and reversibility of the action. Enforce scoped permissions at the execution boundary and separate proposal from authority to enact consequential changes. Preserve a named maintainer and time to learn, so ownership includes practical understanding. A convincing mistake, outside production

In an isolated exercise, give the team a plausible retry change with a consequential flaw, such as a missing retry limit. It looks reasonable and its happy-path tests pass, but a dependency slowdown causes repeated calls. This is a fictional scenario to adapt, not an incident report. Include useful and mediocre recommendations as well, and compare with a relevant baseline. A single deliberately bad case is not a full evaluation of joint performance.

Observe whether the team finds the relevant evidence, detects the flaw and can reach someone with authority and capacity to act. Exercise the actual stop mechanism: pause new work, halt further rollout or revoke the tool's ability to act. Scoped credentials, bounded execution and separation between proposal and execution must make the limit enforceable. A written instruction to stop is insufficient if the system can continue acting.

Practise the fallback or rollback and confirm user-visible recovery. A code rollback may not reverse data changes or remove a backlog, so test restore or reconciliation where necessary. Start with limited exposure and service-specific observation when the change eventually enters the ordinary release process. Update the probes and runbook to match the changed service behaviour.

Observe joint performance

Record useful outcomes, checking effort, consequential-error detection, and the time to notice, intervene and restore. Use the findings to change the workflow, training, permissions or recovery mechanism. Repeat evaluation after material changes in models, tools, dependencies or staffing. The aim is sufficient margin and recoverability. Operational signals remain proxies for the actual functional limit, and an SLO error budget is not Rasmussen's error margin.
