Economic Boundary

External conditions change what the business can sustain.

More room to operate

  • Stronger demand and better pricing power
  • Fresh funding or cheaper financing
  • Lower energy, supplier or infrastructure costs

Less room to operate

  • Market slowdown, churn or price competition
  • Costlier credit or funding that dries up
  • Higher supplier costs or currency shocks

Sustainable capacity changes the boundary.
A tighter internal budget changes the target.

Model: Rasmussen (1997), Figures 1 & 3 · Business examples: this talk’s application.

These are common external conditions, not measurements of a universal boundary. Holding the work and commitments broadly constant, stronger sustainable revenue, accessible capital or a lower sustainable cost base can expand financial viability. Losing them can contract it. Favorable demand also brings work: growth does not automatically increase every margin. An internal cost target or budget cut is a management decision; it is not itself the actual economic-failure constraint. Watch actual cash, obligations and value.

Workload Boundary

The sustainable capacity to do and understand the work can change.

More room to operate

  • More trained people and relevant expertise
  • Better tools and reliable toil reduction
  • Protected focus, recovery time and knowledge sharing

Less room to operate

  • Attrition, layoffs or unavailable specialists
  • Fatigue, burnout and constant interruptions
  • Fragile tooling and lost operational knowledge

Less effective capacity shortens the playing surface.
More incoming work also moves the operating point.

Model: Rasmussen (1997), Figure 3 · Capacity examples: this talk’s application.

Holding the kind and quality of required work broadly constant, usable expert capacity can change the sustainable workload limit. Headcount is an imperfect proxy: new hires need onboarding, relevant expertise, tools and coordination. Greater raw demand is not itself a capacity expansion; it can instead move the operating point toward the workload constraint. Automation can remove toil but adds maintenance and exception work. Burnout and lost knowledge can reduce future effective capacity. These examples are mechanisms for discussion, not a numerical mapping from staff count to diagram geometry.

The hand denotes the gradient toward least effort, not a moral judgment. Better technology and automation can remove repeatable toil and improve assurance. The acceptable-performance boundary is defined by the system's required functions and tolerated harms. Not every crossing is an immediate catastrophe: an unacceptable outcome can be local or escalate through dependencies. Availability, data integrity, confidentiality and safe recovery are software examples. Reputation, commercial losses and regulatory consequences can follow that failure; they are not separate Rasmussen boundaries. Formal SLOs and compliance checks are useful proxies, not an exact picture of the true functional limit. Crossing the dotted perceived boundary enters the protected error margin; it does not prove a failure has already occurred. The model does not provide a measurable distance to catastrophe.

The dotted line represents a fallible perceived limit. Crossing it consumes the deliberately protected error margin; failure is not inevitable at that point. An alert does not push the operating point back. It needs timely interpretation, authority to slow or change work and people with tools to execute a response. Capacity, efficiency and redundancy may improve feasible performance, but need realistic load, failure and recovery testing: correlated dependencies can defeat nominal redundancy. Rasmussen’s framework identifies what to examine. Work with LLM tools needs fresh evidence.

Rasmussen treats human and organisational behaviour as constitutive of the socio-technical system. People and organisations are adaptive components and controllers within it, not an external human input. The paper argues that work migrates under local pressures and that control depends on objectives, feedback, competence, coordination, and commitment. Starting state: The established point is contextual adaptation. Locally sensible behaviour can preserve control, but interactions among adaptations can also create the conditions for failure. Reveal 1: Early LLM evidence shows real but uneven changes. A 2025 CHI survey of 319 knowledge workers found that critical-thinking effort shifted toward verification, response integration, and task stewardship; higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical scrutiny. A randomized study of 758 consultants found faster, higher-quality work on tasks within the model's capability frontier, but lower correctness on a task outside it. Other workplace studies report heterogeneous gains by task and experience. Reveal 2: The system-level question remains open. We need to re-measure problem framing, attention and verification, skill retention under pressure, communication of intent, authority, and feedback fidelity across the control hierarchy. Current task-level studies do not yet establish the accumulated effect on a whole socio-technical system. Reveal 3: This is a transferability and revalidation claim, not a claim that Rasmussen's framework or all earlier human-factors research is obsolete. The framework tells us what to examine when technology changes the behaviour-shaping conditions. “No control system will perform better than its measuring channel.” Jens Rasmussen, 1997
Rasmussen's control structure is the distributed network of decision makers, goals, constraints, information, competence, and authority that controls the work. AI is not automatically a new controller and it is not a fourth boundary. It enters the channels that existing controllers use. On the feedback side, AI can gather information, summarize evidence, find contradictions, and support testing. It can also compress uncertainty into a fluent account that looks more directly observed than it really is. On the control side, AI can present options, draft decisions, and turn loosely stated intent into detailed output. That can increase useful capacity. It can also amplify a preference beyond what the communicator intended or let plausible wording substitute for explicit criteria and verification. “Least effort” is not a moral claim about laziness. It describes normal local adaptation under competing goals and limited attention. AI output does not move the system merely by existing. The operating state moves when people or automation admit and enact the resulting decisions. This risk is conditional. AI can strengthen the safety counter-gradient when it improves testing, diagnosis, challenge, and feedback. Risk rises when generation and action become easier faster than assurance and correction. Then the actual operating state can move closer to a functional limit while the control structure's picture of the system remains behind. This is the preceding slide's feedback-to-action problem applied to economic control. Rasmussen provides the adaptation and control framework. The AI economics and response examples here are this talk's application, not findings about AI in the 1997 paper.

Figure 3 separates the economic-failure constraint from management's pressure toward efficiency. Manuscript pp. 9–11 explain how local adaptations under cost and effort criteria can interact and erode shared defences without immediate visible failure. Page 12 emphasizes visible constraints and coping skills. Pages 15–18 connect effective control to usable objectives, current state information, practical competence and resources. Page 27 requires communication of changes and their implications across levels.

Separate three mechanisms. More consumption or commitments can move the operating state toward economic failure if their cost outpaces the value and financial capacity supporting them. Sustainable savings can expand the viable operating space, while reduced revenue, funding or runway can contract it. Forecasts provide feedback. Budgets and quotas are management setpoints or decision thresholds. A revised spending target does not by itself change the actual viability constraint. Figure 3's dotted perceived boundary and error margin appear only on the performance side.

Lower cost per generation can help, but total economics also depend on activity. Additional workflows, longer contexts, retries or agent loops can consume the saving. Include model use, platform commitments, integration, tools and data, evaluation, human verification, rework and recovery in the outcome cost. This is a conditional mechanism, not a prediction that cheaper AI must increase total cost.

Chapter 5 adds the work-transfer question: who receives the saving, who absorbs the new work, and when does that work become visible? Section 5.2.4 describes costs deferred in time or transferred to others. Section 5.5.2 asks evaluations to include future adaptability, coupling, congestion and conflict. Track authoring savings alongside reviewer, maintainer and on-call costs; a local saving is not sufficient evidence of a net workflow saving.

Organizations can reduce low-value demand, cap unproductive loops, reduce scope, or route and cache work where validated quality permits. They can also use AI to improve testing and diagnosis, increasing useful assurance capacity. Financial feedback must reach people with the authority, competence and resources to act. Teams need to communicate effects on review load and recovery capability as well as spending.

The transfer path is conditional. Holding output constant while removing relevant expertise or review can make the remaining work exceed available capacity. If teams respond by thinning assurance or postponing maintenance, performance margin can shrink while the visible cost metric improves. The efficiency response seeks economic viability, but its side effects can consume other margins. Reassess scope, capacity and outcomes together. Preserve the ability to verify work and recover.

Rasmussen's error margin is the protected buffer between the dotted perceived or marginal boundary and the actual functional performance limit. Crossing the dotted line starts consuming that buffer; it does not establish that harm has occurred. The margin is exhausted at the functional limit. It is not a fourth constraint, a measurement of free staff capacity, or the operating point's distance from any chosen center.

Our application of Chapter 5 is to preserve the means of noticing and responding before that margin is exhausted. Sections 5.3.2–5.3.5 describe difficulties recognizing plausible errors and interpreting uncertain capability. A persuasive explanation is not automatically independent evidence. Section 5.1's scenario describes responsibility without adequate resources and decay of alternative methods. Sections 5.4–5.5 support challenging exercises and continued assurance.

Notice requires usable signals and timely interpretation. Intervention requires evidence, authority, tools and remaining response capacity. Recovery requires contextual knowledge and fallbacks that people can actually use under pressure. A written rollback procedure alone does not demonstrate that capability. Neither the boundary nor awareness of it automatically pushes the operating state back. Corrective action restores sufficient margin and recoverability, not a preferred geometric center.

The AWS 19–20 October 2025 disruption is a supporting example of conventional automation, with no reported generative AI involvement. A stale DNS-plan check and later EC2 recovery congestion illustrate changing state, coupled automation and recovery demand. AWS announced recovery testing and incoming-work controls. The report does not establish that all announced work was complete. Use it as a mechanism example, not as evidence of an AI-caused incident.

Keep the four audience themes tied to one model. Economic failure, unacceptable workload and functionally unacceptable performance constrain the feasible operating space. The error margin is the protected performance-side buffer, not an additional constraint. This closing arrangement is the talk's synthesis; Chapter 5 does not use Rasmussen's model.

Economic: evaluate cost per completed, verified, valuable outcome. Include the work transferred to reviewers, maintainers and responders, along with future coupling and recovery costs. Budgets and quotas guide control; they do not themselves define the actual viability constraint.

Workload: match admitted work to focused review capacity and contextual knowledge. Change scope, timing or demand when verification cannot keep pace. Queue growth does not alone prove a defect increase; risk additionally depends on adaptations that shed assurance.

Performance: evaluate people and tools together across good, mediocre and poor outputs, including convincing mistakes. Repeat appropriate evaluation as models, workflow, staffing and dependencies change. Operational signals remain imperfect proxies for the functional limit.

Error Margin: maintain usable signals, authority to intervene and practiced recovery. The margin does not act on its own. Timely interpretation and a capable response let feedback restore margin and recoverability.

Sense, Constrain, Adapt and Correct remain useful action verbs supporting these themes, rather than a second audience framework. Use AI to strengthen testing, diagnosis and verification where joint evidence supports it. The desired outcome is sufficient margin, bounded excursions and recoverability, with no privileged geometric center.
