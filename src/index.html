Resilience Engineering

Build and sustain adaptive capacity in socio-technical systems, both technical and non-technical, ensuring they can absorb and recover safely from incidents, anomalies and changing pressures.

  • Sustain the ability to adapt as conditions change, in everyday work and surprise.
  • Maintain expertise, coordination, time, resources and authority. Adaptive capacity is more than spare infrastructure.
  • Prepare for events beyond what was anticipated. Learn how people already keep things working.
  • This definition is our synthesis, not a quotation or a promise of immunity.

Economic Boundary

External conditions change what the business can sustain.

More room to operate

  • 📈Stronger demand and better pricing power
  • 💰Fresh funding or cheaper financing
  • 🏭Lower energy, supplier or infrastructure costs

Less room to operate

  • 📉Market slowdown, churn or price competition
  • 🏦Costlier credit or funding that dries up
  • 💱Higher supplier costs or currency shocks

Greater financial capacity expands room to operate, tighter budgets increase pressure to reduce costs.

  • Separate financial viability from a management budget target. Revising a target does not change the actual constraint.
  • Sustainable revenue, funding and lower costs can expand viable space. Growth also brings work and commitments.
  • More consumption can move the operating point toward failure when costs outpace value. Watch cash, obligations and value, not spending alone.

Cutting waste can reduce costs, but cutting expertise or maintenance can leave less room to operate safely.

  • A forecast is a signal. A budget is a target. Someone with authority chooses the response.
  • Remove waste and low-value work before cutting expertise, maintenance or recovery capacity. These examples are possible responses, not recommendations for staffing cuts.
  • Count verification, maintenance and on-call costs alongside authoring savings. Ask who receives the saving, who inherits the work and when the cost appears.
  • Keep financial decisions connected to their workload and performance effects.

Workload Boundary

The sustainable capacity to do and understand the work can change.

More room to operate

  • 🎓More trained people and relevant expertise
  • 🛠️Better tools and reliable toil reduction
  • 🕒Protected focus, recovery time and knowledge sharing

Less room to operate

  • Attrition, layoffs or unavailable specialists
  • 🥵Fatigue, burnout and constant interruptions
  • 🔥Fragile tooling and lost operational knowledge

Less capacity moves the boundary inward, more work can push the operating point toward it.

  • Capacity means relevant expertise, focused time, context and workable tools. Headcount alone is a weak proxy.
  • More demand moves the operating point. More usable capacity can expand the sustainable workload limit.
  • Automation may remove toil while adding maintenance and exception work. Onboarding, coordination, fatigue and lost knowledge also affect capacity.
  • Protect focus, contextual knowledge and recovery. The diagram is conceptual, not a staffing-to-distance calculation.

Reducing toil can preserve control, but skipping checks saves effort now and creates work later.

  • Least effort is normal adaptation under competing goals, not laziness. Good automation can preserve control.
  • Watch queue age, interruptions and recovery time. Review findings are quality feedback, not universally leading indicators. Discuss fatigue with people rather than diagnosing from one metric.
  • Match admitted work to focused verification capacity. Rising queues indicate delay, not automatically more defects.
  • Change scope or timing, preserve source evidence, and give reviewers time and authority to pause or reject. The review-load ratio is this talk’s heuristic, not a validated universal measure.

Boundary of
Acceptable Performance

Crossing the functional limit means unacceptable outcomes.

Failure in the service

  • 🚨Outages and cascading system failures
  • 🔓Lost, corrupted or exposed customer data
  • ⏳Recovery that cannot restore service in time

Consequences

  • 🚧Customer operations disrupted, safety put at risk
  • 💔Trust and reputation damaged
  • ⚖️Contractual losses or regulatory consequences

Can people, tools and workflows anticipate and adapt to failure, ensuring system capacity remains resilient to performance degradation?

  • The functional limit concerns required outcomes and tolerated harms. A crossing need not be catastrophic. SLOs and compliance checks are imperfect proxies.
  • Entering the error margin does not prove failure. The diagram gives no measurable distance to catastrophe.
  • Test people, tools and workflow together across good and poor output, then repeat as conditions change. Compare verified outcomes, checking effort, error detection and recovery with a relevant baseline.
  • Morey et al. (2025): simulated clinical cases, 450 nursing students and 12 nurses. Recommendation correctness mattered. Added explanations showed no significant effect here. This is not an LLM software-risk estimate.

Sustain adaptive capacity by turning signals into effective action

  • The perceived limit is fallible. Entering the margin is a warning, not inevitable failure. An error budget is not a geometric measure of this margin.
  • Alerts need interpretation, authority, tools and remaining capacity for action. Awareness alone restores nothing.
  • Practise diagnosis, bounded changes and recovery under realistic pressure. A written rollback procedure or nominal redundancy does not establish usable capability.
  • If using the AWS October 2025 example: conventional automation, no reported generative AI involvement. Delayed DNS plans and congested EC2 recovery illustrate control problems. Announced corrective work was not all established as complete.

What happens as AI infiltrates every part of the system?

  • The AI artwork still represents one whole-system state. The reference ball marks a starting position, not a second current state or an inherently safe centre.
  • AI can reshape feedback, summaries, options and actions used by existing controllers. It is not automatically a new controller.
  • Plausible ≠ measured. Fluent ≠ intended. Output affects operations when people or automation admit and enact it.
  • Ask whether testing, understanding and correction keep pace with easier generation and action. AI can strengthen assurance too.

AI has to be paid for

Cheaper generation can still mean higher total cost.

The bill grows

  • 📈47% of surveyed tech leaders reported AI spending over plan. ETR, 2026
  • 🪫Uber’s annual AI budget was exhausted by April. CTO interview, 2026
  • 🔁More seats, agent loops and adoption add demand.

Money moves

  • 🏗️Cut infrastructure refresh to fund AI overruns: 49% of firms reallocating budgets. ETR, 2026
  • 🏦Bring in outside capital: Meta’s $27bn Hyperion venture with Blue Owl. 2025
  • 🎯Redirect investment toward AI products with stronger expected returns.

Savings here can add work elsewhere. Who covers it and at what cost?

  • ETR: 1,636 technology leaders, June–July 2026. The 47% reported spending above plan. The 49% applies only to firms reallocating budgets for overruns, not everyone surveyed. Budget choices do not establish reliability harm.
  • Uber: executive account, not audited finances. Meta: $27bn Hyperion development financing, not an annual AI bill, with future obligations. Portfolio reprioritization is illustrative.
  • Unit savings can be absorbed by more use. Count verified outcome cost, integration, review, rework and recovery.
  • Remove low-value demand and bound loops. Savings transfer pressure when commitments remain but expertise or assurance is cut. This is the talk’s software application, not Rasmussen’s AI finding.

How does commercial AI evolve from here, and what are the risks?

  • Tokens measure usage, not useful outcomes. Tokenmaxxing leaderboard spending is an API-equivalent estimate, not reconciled bills. These are LLM tokens, not cryptocurrency.
  • Metering counts usage against allowances. Cost-oriented routing chooses cheaper models and may trade off quality. Neither alone proves deliberate degradation or rent extraction.
  • Cursor’s August 2025 announcement planned metered Auto usage from renewals after September 15. It does not independently confirm every subscriber’s rollout.
  • Evaluate routing against actual work. Ask about outcome cost, portability and alternatives before dependence grows. The following slide examines pricing power, not an inevitable path for every provider.

The erosion of expertise

“However, all of our lessons learned in cognitive systems engineering and complexity theory imply that more automation may result in simplifications, but they will hide deeper complexity, and at those moments where resilience and high-performance are needed, there will have to be greater operator expertise.”

This is not new

Lisanne Bainbridge (1983), Ironies of Automation.

  • Hoffman et al.: automation can simplify the surface while hiding complexity that demands greater expertise during difficult conditions.
  • Bainbridge, 1983: people remain responsible for abnormal conditions while losing practice in the skills needed to diagnose and recover.
  • Her evidence concerns industrial control and flight decks. Applying it to LLMs is a historical analogy, not an AI experiment.
  • Preserve opportunities to practise, understand and intervene. Expertise loss is a risk, not an inevitable consequence of AI.
👩🏿 👨🏼 🧑🏽 🧕🏻
  • The system stays complex even when the people responsible can no longer explain it.
  • Less understanding means fewer options for recognising trouble and intervening effectively.
  • The shrugging and fire illustrate a possible loss of expertise and control, not evidence that all AI use causes failure.

Tool vs Operator

  • 🧰

    AI assists the operator

    Explore, diagnose and test, with people checking the result.

  • 🎛️

    AI acts in the system

    Bound its permissions and pace, retain authority to stop and recover.

Test people, tools and workflow together.

  • Assisting and acting create different control needs. Bound permissions and pace, observe effects, and retain authority to interrupt and recover.
  • Evaluate people, tools and workflow together against relevant baselines, including helpful and misleading AI output.
  • Clinical joint-performance research motivates this question. It does not estimate software or LLM risk.
  • Benefit depends on actual use and evaluation, not the label “tool” or “operator”.

We have to adapt

  • 💸

    Economic: Count generation, verification, operation and switching costs.

  • 😢

    Workload: Match AI output to the team’s ability to understand and check it.

  • 💥

    Performance: Test the work, including plausible AI mistakes and recovery.

  • Error margin: Give people time, expertise and authority to turn signals into action.

Local savings can create pressure elsewhere.

  • Return to three constraints: economic viability, acceptable workload and acceptable performance.
  • Savings in one place can transfer checking, maintenance and recovery work elsewhere. Count the whole workflow.
  • Error margin is the protected performance-side buffer, not an SLO error budget or a fourth constraint.
  • Protect those interpreting warnings and owning corrective action. Adapt these responses using local evidence as conditions change.

Humans are adaptable

Expertise, coordination and judgment help us respond when plans no longer fit.

Use AI to strengthen our capacity to understand, act and recover.

  • Develop adaptive capacity before surprise, and renew it through everyday work.
  • Expertise, shared understanding and coordination let people revise plans when familiar responses stop fitting.
  • They also need time, resources and authority. Human adaptability does not absorb unlimited pressure.
  • Use AI to strengthen understanding, effective action and recovery. Judge sustained joint performance, not output alone.
