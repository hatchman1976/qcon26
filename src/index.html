Session: Adapt or Drift: Resilience Engineering When AI Moves the Operating Point.

We have more capability than ever before

But less certainty we’re in control

Decades of research on sociotechnical systems and complexity still explain these risks.

Will our ability to notice, understand and correct keep pace?

AI is changing what we can do and how fast we can do it. Rasmussen’s model still helps us understand the pressures. The question is whether our ability to notice, understand and correct can keep pace.

AI can radically change how work gets done while still fitting familiar patterns of adaptation under pressure. But it can also influence what people accept as evidence and how intent becomes action. That changes the mechanisms we use to understand and control the system.

The framework can remain useful while our assumptions need rechecking. Decades of research give us a lens. They don’t guarantee that familiar review processes or feedback loops will remain adequate.

Rasmussen supplies the boundary and control framework. Applying it to AI’s tempo, verification demand and feedback delay is this talk’s synthesis. Evaluate whether people, tools and workflow retain the capacity to intervene and recover. AI can also improve assurance.

Resilience Engineering

Build and sustain adaptive capacity in socio-technical systems, both technical and non-technical, ensuring they can absorb and recover safely from incidents, anomalies and changing pressures.

Build the ability to adapt before it is needed

The opening definition is this talk’s synthesis, not a quotation. Resilience Engineering is the study and deliberate support of the capacities that help socio-technical systems continue to perform as conditions change. Sustaining that ability means maintaining expertise, coordination, time, resources and authority to act, and renewing them as the system evolves. Adaptive capacity is not simply spare infrastructure capacity.

Incidents and anomalies make adaptation visible, but it also occurs in everyday work. Keeping functioning can involve changing plans, priorities or ways of working. This does not promise immunity from disruption or unlimited capacity to absorb any event. Prepare for situations that exceed what was anticipated, and learn how people already prevent difficulties from becoming worse.

John Allspaw’s March 6, 2019 presentation abstract also places investment in adaptive capacity at the centre of the field. It remains a background source for the definition: Amplifying Sources of Resilience.

Where does control live?

Social control hierarchy Rasmussen’s structural hierarchy

The social control hierarchy surrounds the people and technology doing the work. Research disciplines and environmental stressors cross its levels.

Control is distributed rather than owned by any single actor, and information must travel upward while decisions and constraints travel downward. Each layer adapts on a different timescale.

Goals, evidence and authority

The control structure is the distributed network of decision makers, goals, constraints, information, competence and authority around the work. Observations and outcomes inform decisions, and decisions and constraints change the work. Feedback must reach actors with the resources and authority to respond.

When AI enters these channels, re-examine problem framing, attention, verification, skill retention under pressure, communicated intent and feedback fidelity across the hierarchy. Task-level studies do not establish the accumulated effects on a whole socio-technical system.

Plain-language script

“The economic boundary marks where we can no longer pay the bills. The workload boundary marks where people cannot keep going. The performance boundary marks where the system can no longer do its job acceptably or safely. We cannot see that line exactly, so the dotted line gives us a margin for error.

This dot is not a person. It is the whole system — people, software, and rules — working right now. The dim dot marks the starting reference position, not a second current operating state. The shaded area is the playing surface, with the error margin shown in orange.

Economic and workload limits can change as financial and human capacity change. Inward shifts reduce the playing surface; outward shifts expand it.

Pressure for efficiency and less effort can draw normal adaptations, and the whole system's operating state, toward the performance boundary.

The charts stand for pressure to reduce cost, which a downturn can intensify. The hands stand for ways people reduce effort under workload; the Blocker Lemmings show safety action near the error margin: pause or redirect work to preserve margin. Rasmussen describes actors adapting within the work-space, encountering an effort gradient while management supplies a cost gradient. The charts and hands are scattered through that space to illustrate local adaptations, not measured locations of individual decisions in Figure 3.

The economic-side arrow shows the gradient toward cost efficiency; the workload-side arrow shows the gradient toward least effort. Both point toward the acceptable-performance side. Figure 3 and its adjacent discussion support these directions of migration. The arrow endpoints are illustrative rather than measured positions or fixed targets.

Everyone makes sensible choices. The business asks for cheaper and faster. People make work easier. Together, those pressures can move the dot toward the performance edge.

There is no magic safe spot in the middle. The lines do not push the dot back. Any line can move as demand, staffing, technology, or AI changes.

That is Rasmussen's warning: failure does not require one stupid decision. Normal adaptations can slowly move the system toward an edge nobody can see exactly.”

Core point: one current operating state moves within economic, workload, and acceptable-performance constraints; pressures move it, while sensing and control preserve margin and recoverability.

Economic Boundary

External conditions change what the business can sustain.

More room to operate

  • 📈Stronger demand and better pricing power
  • 💰Fresh funding or cheaper financing
  • 🏭Lower energy, supplier or infrastructure costs

Less room to operate

  • 📉Market slowdown, churn or price competition
  • 🏦Costlier credit or funding that dries up
  • 💱Higher supplier costs or currency shocks

Greater financial capacity expands room to operate, tighter budgets increase pressure to reduce costs.

These are common external conditions, not measurements of a universal boundary. Holding the work and commitments broadly constant, stronger sustainable revenue, accessible capital or a lower sustainable cost base can expand financial viability. Losing them can contract it. Favorable demand also brings work: growth does not automatically increase every margin. An internal cost target or budget cut is a management decision; it is not itself the actual economic-failure constraint. Watch actual cash, obligations and value.

Activity, capacity and control

More consumption or commitments can move the operating state toward economic failure when costs outpace value and supporting financial capacity. Sustainable savings can expand viable operating space; reduced revenue, funding or runway can contract it. Forecasts provide feedback, and budgets or quotas are management targets and decision thresholds. Revised targets do not by themselves change the actual viability constraint. Rasmussen draws the perceived boundary and error margin only on the performance side.

A forecast is a signal and a budget is a target. An actor with authority decides what to do. These are possible organizational responses, not recommendations to reduce staffing or resilience. Unit economics should include human verification and recovery. Right-sizing and retiring unused services can preserve performance; cutting necessary expertise, maintenance or redundancy while retaining commitments can consume performance margin. Validate the effect of savings across the whole workflow. Existing incidents or realized losses are lagging outcomes; forecast cash and projected margins are earlier financial signals.

Account for transferred work

Chapter 5 §5.2.4 (printed p. 81; supplied PDF p. 89) asks who receives a saving, who absorbs the work, and when it becomes visible. Workload Boundary

The sustainable capacity to do and understand the work can change.

More room to operate

  • 🎓More trained people and relevant expertise
  • 🛠️Better tools and reliable toil reduction
  • 🕒Protected focus, recovery time and knowledge sharing

Less room to operate

  • Attrition, layoffs or unavailable specialists
  • 🥵Fatigue, burnout and constant interruptions
  • 🔥Fragile tooling and lost operational knowledge

Less capacity moves the boundary inward, more work can push the operating point toward it.

Holding the kind and quality of required work broadly constant, usable expert capacity can change the sustainable workload limit. Headcount is an imperfect proxy: new hires need onboarding, relevant expertise, tools and coordination. Greater raw demand is not itself a capacity expansion; it can instead move the operating point toward the workload constraint. Automation can remove toil but adds maintenance and exception work. Burnout and lost knowledge can reduce future effective capacity. These examples are mechanisms for discussion, not a numerical mapping from staff count to diagram geometry.

Focused verification capacity

Effective capacity means relevant expertise, focused time and enough context for this work, not merely assigned headcount. A reviewer can be allocated to many tasks yet lack usable time to assess this change. Protect contextual knowledge and recovery; changing demand or removing expert capacity can also change the verification effort required per item.

Can people, tools and workflows anticipate and adapt to failure, ensuring system capacity remains resilient to performance degradation and recovers?

The acceptable-performance boundary is defined by the system's required functions and tolerated harms. Not every crossing is an immediate catastrophe: an unacceptable outcome can be local or escalate through dependencies. Availability, data integrity, confidentiality and safe recovery are software examples. Reputation, commercial losses and regulatory consequences can follow that failure; they are not separate Rasmussen boundaries. Formal SLOs and compliance checks are useful proxies, not an exact picture of the true functional limit. Crossing the dotted perceived boundary enters the protected error margin; it does not prove a failure has already occurred. The model does not provide a measurable distance to catastrophe.

Joint performance over the lifecycle

Evaluate people, tools and workflow together across good, mediocre and poor AI output. A model benchmark alone cannot establish operational joint performance. Chapter 5 §5.5.3 (printed pp. 85–86; PDF pp. 93–94) calls for evaluation situated in the actual work and repeated through the lifecycle.

Compare verified outcomes, revision and checking effort with a relevant baseline. Include a plausible consequential mistake in a controlled exercise: can people access the evidence, detect it and correct it before enactment? Measure outcome quality, verification effort, consequential-error detection, response time and successful recovery. Re-evaluate when models, workflow, staffing or dependencies materially change. These are proposed engineering measures rather than a universal score; operational signals remain imperfect proxies for the true functional limit.

Morey, Rayo and Woods (2025) studied predictive clinical AI in simulated patient cases with 450 nursing students and 12 licensed nurses. Recommendation correctness improved or degraded joint performance. Added explanations did not significantly change those effects in this experiment. It motivates the evaluation design; it is not an LLM software-risk estimate or evidence that explanations never help.

Sustain adaptive capacity turning signals into effective action

The dotted line represents a fallible perceived limit. Crossing it consumes the deliberately protected error margin; failure is not inevitable at that point. An alert does not push the operating point back. It needs timely interpretation, authority to slow or change work and people with tools to execute a response. Capacity, efficiency and redundancy may improve feasible performance, but need realistic load, failure and recovery testing: correlated dependencies can defeat nominal redundancy. Canaries, least privilege, independent review, rollback and restore exercises preserve bounded excursions and recovery. An error budget is a service-management mechanism, not a geometric measurement of Rasmussen's error margin.

Notice, intervene and recover

Deliberate interventions pause or redirect work to preserve margin. Someone needs the evidence, authority and capacity to act.

Notice requires usable signals and timely interpretation. Intervention requires evidence, authority, tools and remaining response capacity. Recovery requires contextual knowledge and fallbacks that people can use under pressure. A written rollback procedure does not establish that capability. Awareness and the margin itself exert no automatic restoring force; the objective is sufficient margin, bounded excursions and recoverability, not a preferred geometric center.

Exercise stale observations, scarce time, excess review demand and unavailable supporting services outside production. Assess whether the team can pause, reduce scope and recover. What changes when AI enters the work?

The operating point represents the whole socio-technical system's current state. The AI artwork is shorthand for AI-mediated work at that same state, not a second operating point, a person or a fourth constraint. The reference ball marks the starting state, not an ideal or inherently safe location.

Ask what changes in cost, effort, evidence and recovery when AI enters the work.

AI in the feedback and action channels

AI is not automatically a new controller. It influences channels used by existing controllers. On the feedback side it can gather and summarize observations, find contradictions and support testing; fluent summaries can also conceal uncertainty. On the action side it can present options, draft decisions and translate intent into output; plausible detail can exceed the communicator’s intended criteria.

AI output changes the operating state when people or automation admit and enact the resulting decisions, not merely when output is generated. The risk is conditional: AI can strengthen testing, diagnosis, challenge and verification. Risk rises when generation and action become easier faster than assurance and correction.

Among respondents reallocating to cover overruns, 61% cut contractor/consultant spending, 49% legacy infrastructure modernization or hardware refresh, 36% non-AI licenses and 28% internal headcount budgets. This brings in outside capital rather than cutting another internal budget, with future lease and guarantee obligations. The amount is project development cost, not Meta's annual AI bill. The portfolio-return example is an illustrative management choice. These cases distinguish reduced infrastructure investment, external financing and product reprioritization. Cost control can preserve other margins by dropping low-value work. It transfers pressure when required output stays constant but relevant expertise, maintenance or assurance disappears. More AI expenditure moves the operating state; budgets remain targets, not an extra dotted economic boundary.

Whole-workflow economics

Include model use, platform commitments, integration, tools and data, evaluation, human verification, rework and recovery in outcome cost. Longer contexts, retries, agent loops and additional workflows can consume unit-cost savings. This is conditional, not a prediction that cheaper AI must increase spending. Evaluate work transferred or deferred to reviewers, maintainers and responders, along with future coupling, congestion and recovery costs.

Preserve capacity by reducing low-value demand, bounding loops, changing scope and routing or caching where verified quality permits. Organizations may also redirect portfolios toward products with stronger expected returns, as the visible example suggests. Keep verification and recovery funded. AI can improve tests and diagnosis as well as generation. If required output stays constant while relevant expertise or review is removed, workload can rise and performance margin can shrink.

Token consumption, dependence and platform power

Tokenmaxxing turns LLM-agent consumption into a public scoreboard. The tokenmaxxing.sh leaderboard ranks token counts and estimated API-equivalent spending. Those estimates are not reconciled bills, and token consumption does not measure useful outcomes, productivity or quality. These are language-model usage tokens, not cryptocurrency. The site was checked on October 27, 2026.

Doctorow’s enshittification model describes two-sided platforms. They first offer users substantial value, then shift value toward business customers such as advertisers and sellers, then squeeze both groups to retain more surplus for the platform. Business customers are distinct from the platform owner. This is a conditional analogy about AI dependence and pricing power, not a claim about every provider or measured model quality.

Cursor’s August 12, 2025 announcement describes a change in metering. Individual Auto usage had been unlimited since June. The announcement said it would count against included monthly usage at token rates from each user’s next renewal after September 15, 2025. Some annual renewals could fall in 2026. This dates the announcement and planned transition, not independently confirmed rollout for every subscriber. This does not establish deliberate lock-in, reduced model quality or rent extraction.

Optimization describes a business routing users’ requests to cheaper models to reduce costs. Microsoft’s model-router documentation describes a Cost mode that favours smaller, cheaper models, with possible quality trade-offs on complex prompts. Routing requires evaluation against the actual workload. Cheaper routing alone does not demonstrate deliberate degradation or Doctorow’s full platform cycle.

The parallel is a risk to examine: when workflows depend on a provider, that provider controls the meter and can change the terms. Token leaderboards can reward consumption while the organization needs useful, verified outcomes. These documented examples do not establish that every AI provider has completed Doctorow’s cycle. Ask about switching costs, portability, alternative suppliers and outcome cost before increased use becomes a commitment that is difficult to unwind.

Cloud-market example: CMA, 2025

After Cory Doctorow, Enshittification (2025)

Doctorow describes a two-sided platform moving value from users to business customers, then taking more from both groups. Business customers mean advertisers, sellers or other participants, distinct from the platform owner. The headings are a paraphrase. Applying this pattern to AI is a risk to examine, not a prediction that every provider follows it.

Lower costs do not determine who keeps the savings

Stanford’s AI Index article reports that the price of querying models at GPT-3.5-level MMLU performance fell from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024. Its 2025 report also describes hardware costs per unit of performance declining by 30% annually. These are historical trends, not guarantees about future token prices or total workflow costs.

The cloud analogy concerns competition and switching. For its UK and EEA market definition, the CMA’s July 2025 cloud decision found concentrated supply, sustained AWS and Microsoft returns above their cost of capital, and barriers to switching. Its April 2024 foundation-model review identified risks from control over critical inputs and routes to customers. These findings support questioning how fully efficiency gains reach customers, rather than asserting an inevitable AI oligopoly.

The October 2026 CMA update records improvements, including removal of transfer fees for switching providers, while identifying remaining barriers. Cloud prices have risen for some services and fallen for others. The precedent is imperfect competition, not a claim that cloud prices never fall.

DeepSeek still supplies MIT-licensed model weights and charges for its hosted API. Open weights do not mean free compute. The evidence does not establish that incumbents will push DeepSeek out. The question is whether competing models remain practically accessible and switching remains viable as the market evolves.

Extra checking and context switching can

Workslop is polished-looking output that leaves the recipient to reconstruct missing thinking. The BetterUp/Stanford survey of 1,150 full-time US desk workers found 40% reporting receipt in the prior month, and roughly two hours on average to resolve an incident. These are self-reports, not an objective audit of every AI-generated artifact. The chain is this talk's software-workflow illustration: a person may use an agent to proxy an argument, answer or decision without forming their own understanding. The recipient must check the assumptions and reconcile conflicts. AI can also reduce this effort when used to build comprehension and assurance. Berkeley researchers observed eight months at one 200-person US technology company and conducted more than 40 interviews. Broader task scope, parallel streams and work extending into breaks/evenings intensified work. This motivates burnout risk from reduced recovery, not a universal causal finding. Expertise, familiarity, staffing and the design of handoffs affect who carries the added load; these sources do not prove that lower-skill or less-parallel users invariably suffer more burnout. Completed backlog counts should be read alongside verification time, rework, interruptions, recovery and ability to explain changes. The hand depicts least-effort adaptation. The meat-proxy emoji illustrates an engineer coping with increased workload by relaying plausible AI reasoning, arguments or judgments as their own, without understanding the fundamentals. This can create the appearance of expertise while bypassing the work that develops and exercises it. It can also leave the recipient to verify reasoning the sender cannot explain. Keep a person accountable for understanding, explaining and checking the work they pass onward.

Evidence, capacity and understanding

Least effort describes normal adaptation under competing goals and limited attention, not a moral claim about laziness. Plausible ≠ measured. Fluent ≠ intended. Fast generation does not establish fast verification, integration or maintenance. Conflicting AI proposals still require people to reconstruct context and understand fundamentals. The meat-proxy scenario is this talk’s illustration, not a claim that all AI use reduces expertise. Chapter 5 §§5.3.1–5.3.4 (printed pp. 82–83; PDF pp. 90–91) discusses filtering, revision and plausible errors that need expertise to recognize and correct.

Current LLM evidence shows changes in task pace and cognitive effort that differ by task and experience. Lee et al. (CHI 2025) surveyed 319 knowledge workers: critical-thinking effort shifted toward verification, response integration and task stewardship, while higher confidence in AI was associated with less scrutiny. This is self-reported association, not causal proof. Dell’Acqua et al. randomized 758 consultants: work was faster and higher quality within the model’s capability frontier, but correctness was lower outside it. These are task-specific results, not a universal effect or a whole-system risk estimate. Dillon et al. and Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond provide additional heterogeneous workplace evidence.

Preserve focused attention, contextual knowledge and usable authority to change commitments. Change scope, timing or admitted demand when checking cannot keep pace. AI can improve assurance capacity where joint evidence supports its testing and diagnosis. Demand relative to capacity can increase backlog and delay; defects additionally depend on adaptations that shed assurance. Do not treat generated backlog clearance as verified completion.

The erosion of expertise

“However, all of our lessons learned in cognitive systems engineering and complexity theory imply that more automation may result in simplifications, but they will hide deeper complexity, and at those moments where resilience and high-performance are needed, there will have to be greater operator expertise.”

Accelerated Expertise: Training for High Proficiency in a Complex World, by Robert R. Hoffman, Paul Ward, Paul J. Feltovich, Lia DiBello, Stephen M. Fiore and Dee H. Andrews.

Citation year and publisher follow the publisher's DOI record: 2013, Psychology Press, DOI 10.4324/9780203797327. The print edition carries a 2014 copyright year.

The supplied quotation is attributed to Accelerated Expertise. Use it to discuss how automation can simplify the surface of work while obscuring complexity that demands greater expertise when resilience and high performance are needed. This is a discussion of the risk to expertise, not a measured claim that all AI use erodes it.

The buckets represent accumulating verification and repair work. The Lemming represents the expertise and response capacity needed to protect performance. Cheap generation can move checking, maintenance and recovery work elsewhere, consuming attention and capacity even as visible output increases.

Skills that protect margin

  • 🔎

    Systems understanding

    Trace how AI shifts cost, workload and risk across the system.

  • 🧠

    Critical judgment

    Challenge plausible output and test it against the real work.

  • 🔄

    Adaptation and recovery

    Notice drift, revise mental models and act before margin runs out.

Expertise turns warnings into protective action.

Rasmussen directs attention to how locally reasonable decisions interact across the whole system. AI can change cost, effort and the pace of proposed action. Expertise helps people see those interactions, question plausible explanations and recognise when a plan no longer fits. Protecting margin requires useful feedback and the capacity to act on it. Crossing the dotted line begins consuming the protected buffer before the actual functional boundary. It does not establish that harm has already occurred.

Tool vs Operator

  • 🧰

    AI assists the operator

    Explore, diagnose and test, with people checking the result.

  • 🎛️

    AI acts in the system

    Bound its permissions and pace, retain authority to stop and recover.

Test people, tools and workflow together.

AI can assist diagnosis, testing and explanation, and can also be authorised to act. It can change existing feedback and action channels. The distinction changes what must be bounded and observed. People need useful evidence, practical understanding and enforceable authority to interrupt or recover. Evaluate the joint workflow with relevant baselines and both helpful and misleading outputs. The clinical joint-performance research motivates this question but does not estimate software or LLM risk. Whether an application helps depends on its use and evaluation, not the label “tool” or “operator”.

Sources: Rasmussen (1997), Morey et al. (2025), Rayo et al. (2026), Chapter 5.

We have to adapt

  • 💸

    Economic: Count generation, verification, operation and switching costs.

  • 😢

    Workload: Match AI output to the team’s ability to understand and check it.

  • 💥

    Performance: Test the work, including plausible AI mistakes and recovery.

  • Error margin: Give people time, expertise and authority to turn signals into action.

Local savings can create pressure elsewhere.

The same three constraints remain: economic viability, acceptable workload and acceptable performance. AI can save effort in one place while moving verification, maintenance or recovery work elsewhere. Count the whole workflow and protect the people who interpret warning signals and own corrective action. Error margin is a protected performance-side buffer, not an SLO error budget or a fourth constraint. These proposed responses need local evidence and adjustment as conditions change.

Sources: Rasmussen (1997), Rayo et al. (2026), CMA (2025).

Humans are adaptable

Expertise, coordination and judgment help us respond when plans no longer fit.

Use AI to strengthen our capacity to understand, act and recover.

Adaptive capacity is developed before surprise and renewed through everyday work. Expertise, shared understanding and coordination help people change plans when familiar responses are no longer enough. They also need time, resources and authority to act. AI can contribute to that capacity when its use supports understanding and effective action. The aim is sustained joint performance and recoverability. This is the talk's application of resilience engineering, not a guarantee that humans or tools can absorb unlimited pressure.

Sources: Cook (2000), Allspaw (2019), Woods (2018), Hoffman et al. (2013).
