The dotted line marks a perceived limit inside the actual performance boundary; the gap between them is the error margin. Boundaries constrain the playing surface, but they do not push the point in any direction. The combined tank, hand and chart are this talk's shorthand for actions and pressures: safety interventions, adaptations to reduce effort, and pressure toward cost efficiency. Rasmussen's Figure 3 describes local adaptive variations, effort and cost gradients, and a safety countergradient. Figure 1 shows technological, market and political stressors acting across the socio-technical hierarchy. Local actions and wider pressures interact; an external condition is not necessarily a deliberate action. In this adaptation, changes can shift the operating point or reshape the constraints around it. Actions may arise within a team's immediate work or elsewhere in the control hierarchy. Rasmussen’s framework identifies what to examine. Work with LLM tools needs fresh evidence.

Rasmussen treats human and organisational behaviour as constitutive of the socio-technical system. People and organisations are adaptive components and controllers within it, not an external human input. The paper argues that work migrates under local pressures and that control depends on objectives, feedback, competence, coordination, and commitment. Starting state: The established point is contextual adaptation. Locally sensible behaviour can preserve control, but interactions among adaptations can also create the conditions for failure. Reveal 1: Early LLM evidence shows real but uneven changes. A 2025 CHI survey of 319 knowledge workers found that critical-thinking effort shifted toward verification, response integration, and task stewardship; higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical scrutiny. A randomized study of 758 consultants found faster, higher-quality work on tasks within the model's capability frontier, but lower correctness on a task outside it. Other workplace studies report heterogeneous gains by task and experience. Reveal 2: The system-level question remains open. We need to re-measure problem framing, attention and verification, skill retention under pressure, communication of intent, authority, and feedback fidelity across the control hierarchy. Current task-level studies do not yet establish the accumulated effect on a whole socio-technical system. Reveal 3: This is a transferability and revalidation claim, not a claim that Rasmussen's framework or all earlier human-factors research is obsolete. The framework tells us what to examine when technology changes the behaviour-shaping conditions. “No control system will perform better than its measuring channel.” Jens Rasmussen, 1997
Rasmussen's control structure is the distributed network of decision makers, goals, constraints, information, competence, and authority that controls the work. AI is not automatically a new controller and it is not a fourth boundary. It enters the channels that existing controllers use. On the feedback side, AI can gather information, summarize evidence, find contradictions, and support testing. It can also compress uncertainty into a fluent account that looks more directly observed than it really is. On the control side, AI can present options, draft decisions, and turn loosely stated intent into detailed output. That can increase useful capacity. It can also amplify a preference beyond what the communicator intended or let plausible wording substitute for explicit criteria and verification. “Least effort” is not a moral claim about laziness. It describes normal local adaptation under competing goals and limited attention. AI output does not move the system merely by existing. The operating state moves when people or automation admit and enact the resulting decisions. This risk is conditional. AI can strengthen the safety counter-gradient when it improves testing, diagnosis, challenge, and feedback. Risk rises when generation and action become easier faster than assurance and correction. Then the actual operating state can move closer to a functional limit while the control structure's picture of the system remains behind. Rasmussen's 1997 paper describes behaviour and work activity migrating toward the boundary. “Operating point” is later shorthand used explicitly by Cook and Rasmussen in 2005. This is the preceding slide's feedback-to-action problem applied to economic control. Rasmussen provides the adaptation and control framework. The AI economics and response examples here are this talk's application, not findings about AI in the 1997 paper. Figure 3 separates the economic-failure constraint from management's pressure toward efficiency. Manuscript pp. 9–11 explain how local adaptations under cost and effort criteria can interact and erode shared defences without immediate visible failure. Page 12 emphasizes visible constraints and coping skills. Pages 15–18 connect effective control to usable objectives, current state information, practical competence and resources. Page 27 requires communication of changes and their implications across levels. Separate three mechanisms. More consumption or commitments can move the operating state toward economic failure if their cost outpaces the value and financial capacity supporting them. Sustainable savings can expand the viable operating space, while reduced revenue, funding or runway can contract it. Forecasts provide feedback. Budgets and quotas are management setpoints or decision thresholds. A revised spending target does not by itself change the actual viability constraint. Figure 3's dotted perceived boundary and error margin appear only on the performance side. Lower cost per generation can help, but total economics also depend on activity. Additional workflows, longer contexts, retries or agent loops can consume the saving. Include model use, platform commitments, integration, tools and data, evaluation, human verification, rework and recovery in the outcome cost. This is a conditional mechanism, not a prediction that cheaper AI must increase total cost. Organizations can reduce low-value demand, cap unproductive loops, reduce scope, or route and cache work where validated quality permits. They can also use AI to improve testing and diagnosis, increasing useful assurance capacity. Financial feedback must reach people with the authority, competence and resources to act. Teams need to communicate effects on review load and recovery capability as well as spending. The transfer path is conditional. Holding output constant while removing relevant expertise or review can make the remaining work exceed available capacity. If teams respond by thinning assurance or postponing maintenance, performance margin can shrink while the visible cost metric improves. The efficiency response seeks economic viability, but its side effects can consume other margins. Reassess scope, capacity and outcomes together. Preserve the ability to verify work and recover.
