Boundary of functionally acceptable performance

Boundary to economic failure

Boundary to unacceptable workload

Plain-language script: “This dot is not a person. It is the whole system — people, software, and rules — working right now. It keeps moving as work changes. I am going to draw three chalk lines around it. The first marks where the system can no longer do its job acceptably or safely. The second marks where we can no longer pay the bills. The third marks where people cannot keep going. We cannot see the performance line exactly, so we work from where we think it is. The gap between our perceived line and the real limit is our margin for error. Everyone makes sensible choices. The business asks for cheaper and faster. People make work easier. Together, those pressures can move the dot toward the performance edge. There is no magic safe spot in the middle. The lines do not push the dot back. Any line can move as demand, staffing, technology, or AI changes. That is Rasmussen's warning: failure does not require one stupid decision. Normal adaptations can slowly move the system toward an edge nobody can see exactly.” Adult one-sentence version: one current operating state moves within economic, workload, and acceptable-performance constraints; pressures move it, while sensing and control preserve margin and recoverability. Human behaviour with LLM tools

Rasmussen treats behaviour as part of control. LLM tools change the conditions in which people work.

ESTABLISHED

People adapt work to local conditions.

Those adaptations can maintain control or create conditions for failure.

EARLY EVIDENCE

Studies find changes in task pace and cognitive effort.

Effects differ by task and experience.

LLM new condition

OPEN QUESTION

How do these changes affect judgment, learning, and feedback across the control structure?

?

Rasmussen’s framework identifies what to examine. Work with LLM tools needs fresh evidence.

Rasmussen treats human and organisational behaviour as constitutive of the socio-technical system. People and organisations are adaptive components and controllers within it, not an external human input. The paper argues that work migrates under local pressures and that control depends on objectives, feedback, competence, coordination, and commitment. Starting state: The established point is contextual adaptation. Locally sensible behaviour can preserve control, but interactions among adaptations can also create the conditions for failure. Reveal 1: Early LLM evidence shows real but uneven changes. A 2025 CHI survey of 319 knowledge workers found that critical-thinking effort shifted toward verification, response integration, and task stewardship; higher confidence in AI was associated with less critical scrutiny. A randomized study of 758 consultants found faster, higher-quality work on tasks within the model's capability frontier, but lower correctness on a task outside it. Other workplace studies report heterogeneous gains by task and experience. Reveal 2: The system-level question remains open. We need to re-measure problem framing, attention and verification, skill retention under pressure, communication of intent, authority, and feedback fidelity across the control hierarchy. Current task-level studies do not yet establish the accumulated effect on a whole socio-technical system. Reveal 3: This is a transferability and revalidation claim, not a claim that Rasmussen's framework or all earlier human-factors research is obsolete. The framework tells us what to examine when technology changes the behaviour-shaping conditions. AI can make “generate, accept, act” the path of least effort.

“No control system will perform better than its measuring channel.” Jens Rasmussen, 1997
Rasmussen's control structure is the distributed network of decision makers, goals, constraints, information, competence, and authority that controls the work. AI is not automatically a new controller and it is not a fourth boundary. It enters the channels that existing controllers use. On the feedback side, AI can gather information, summarize evidence, find contradictions, and support testing. It can also compress uncertainty into a fluent account that looks more directly observed than it really is. On the control side, AI can present options, draft decisions, and turn loosely stated intent into detailed output. That can increase useful capacity. It can also amplify a preference beyond what the communicator intended or let plausible wording substitute for explicit criteria and verification. “Least effort” is not a moral claim about laziness. It describes normal local adaptation under competing goals and limited attention. AI output does not move the system merely by existing. The operating state moves when people or automation admit and enact the resulting decisions. This risk is conditional. AI can strengthen the safety counter-gradient when it improves testing, diagnosis, challenge, and feedback. Risk rises when generation and action become easier faster than assurance and correction. Then the actual operating state can move closer to a functional limit while the control structure's picture of the system remains behind. Rasmussen's 1997 paper describes behaviour and work activity migrating toward the boundary. “Operating point” is later shorthand used explicitly by Cook and Rasmussen in 2005.
