# Working idea — Workload constraint

## Slide copy

Workload constraint

**Adaptation can conceal overload—until adaptive capacity is exhausted.**

Demand and capacity:

- Review-load ratio
- Review queue age and work in progress

Compensatory adaptations:

- Context switching and rising after-hours work

Assurance shedding:

- Falling review, testing, and investigation depth
- Bypasses and deferred maintenance

Later human outcomes:

- Burnout
- Sickness absence
- Attrition

**Burnout can warn that performance-control capacity is being lost.**

## Speaker notes

Workload is usually visible to the people absorbing it. It is harder for the organization to see because local adaptation initially preserves output.

Make the leading signals louder while the organization can still respond. Once burnout and attrition become visible, substantial workload margin has already been consumed.

An illustrative review-load ratio is ρreview = λE[S] / C, where λ is admitted reviewable change, E[S] is expert verification effort per change, and C is available verification capacity. This is a capacity heuristic, not a complete queueing model. Under queueing assumptions, saturation predicts backlog and delay—not defects or context switching by itself.

People initially compensate with additional effort, coordination, and attention. When that discretionary adaptive capacity is exhausted, they must shed load.

Safe load shedding reduces scope, limits work in progress, accepts latency, or adds capacity. Risk-transferring load shedding shortens review, defers maintenance, ignores weak signals, and reduces assurance depth.

If economic pressure protects throughput and assurance becomes the pressure-release valve, the operating point can move near the workload boundary toward the joint-constraint region close to unacceptable performance. It is not in two places: one operating state has little remaining margin to both boundaries.
