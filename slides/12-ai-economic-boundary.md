# Slide 12 — AI and the Economic Boundary

## Slide copy

**AI and the economic boundary**

Cheaper generation can still mean higher total cost.

_Recurring theme rail: **Economic** / Workload / Performance / Error Margin._

Starting state:

- **Operating state:** More use and commitments can consume financial margin.
- **Economic boundary:** Sustainable cost and financial capacity determine viability.
- **Economic control:** Forecasts, budgets and quotas guide the response.

Reveal 1 — How organizations cope:

- **Preserve capacity:** Limit low-value use. Route or cache where quality holds. Fund verification and recovery.
- **Transfer pressure:** If output stays fixed while expertise or review are cut, workload can rise and performance margin can shrink.

Reveal 2:

**Measure total cost per completed, verified, valuable outcome.**

Rasmussen (1997), Fig. 3. AI application for this talk.

## Speaker notes

This is the preceding slide's feedback-to-action problem applied to economic control. Rasmussen provides the adaptation and control framework. The AI economics and response examples here are this talk's application, not findings about AI in the 1997 paper.

Figure 3 separates the economic-failure constraint from management's pressure toward efficiency. Manuscript pp. 9–11 explain how local adaptations under cost and effort criteria can interact and erode shared defences without immediate visible failure. Page 12 emphasizes visible constraints and coping skills. Pages 15–18 connect effective control to usable objectives, current state information, practical competence and resources. Page 27 requires communication of changes and their implications across levels.

Separate three mechanisms. More consumption or commitments can move the operating state toward economic failure if their cost outpaces the value and financial capacity supporting them. Sustainable savings can expand the viable operating space, while reduced revenue, funding or runway can contract it. Forecasts provide feedback. Budgets and quotas are management setpoints or decision thresholds. A revised spending target does not by itself change the actual viability constraint. Figure 3's dotted perceived boundary and error margin appear only on the performance side.

Lower cost per generation can help, but total economics also depend on activity. Additional workflows, longer contexts, retries or agent loops can consume the saving. Include model use, platform commitments, integration, tools and data, evaluation, human verification, rework and recovery in the outcome cost. This is a conditional mechanism, not a prediction that cheaper AI must increase total cost.

Chapter 5 adds the work-transfer question: who receives the saving, who absorbs the new work, and when does that work become visible? Section 5.2.4 describes costs deferred in time or transferred to others. Section 5.5.2 asks evaluations to include future adaptability, coupling, congestion and conflict. Track authoring savings alongside reviewer, maintainer and on-call costs; a local saving is not sufficient evidence of a net workflow saving.

Organizations can reduce low-value demand, cap unproductive loops, reduce scope, or route and cache work where validated quality permits. They can also use AI to improve testing and diagnosis, increasing useful assurance capacity. Financial feedback must reach people with the authority, competence and resources to act. Teams need to communicate effects on review load and recovery capability as well as spending.

The transfer path is conditional. Holding output constant while removing relevant expertise or review can make the remaining work exceed available capacity. If teams respond by thinning assurance or postponing maintenance, performance margin can shrink while the visible cost metric improves. The efficiency response seeks economic viability, but its side effects can consume other margins. Reassess scope, capacity and outcomes together. Preserve the ability to verify work and recover.
