## Speaker notes

The three-boundary diagram is the part most people remember, but this is a synthesis paper rather than a single empirical validation of Figure 3.

Rasmussen distinguishes empirical, evolutionary, and analytical risk strategies. Defence in depth matters, but redundant protections can degrade quietly because a local violation may have no immediate visible effect.

The paper reviews human reliability, operator modelling, field evidence, and organisational research. Its direction of travel is away from isolated error counts and toward behaviour in a real work context shaped by workload, time, cost, and competing goals. Risk management becomes a feedback-control problem spanning the whole socio-technical hierarchy.

Decisions are embedded in continuous work. Locally sensible adaptations can interact across controllers and create system-level risk.

Figure 3 is one of eight figures in a 31-page, cross-disciplinary argument. The next slide simplifies the geometry without discarding these mechanisms.

## Accuracy guardrails

- This paper synthesizes research traditions and evidence. It does not report one new experiment validating Figure 3.
- “Human performance under pressure” includes operator studies, event reports, human-reliability research, and organisational adaptation. It should not imply that this article reports a new controlled study.
- Defence in depth is treated critically: protections can degrade without an immediate visible failure.
- Risk analysis is hazard-specific, not one universal method.
- The least-effort gradient describes normal local adaptation, not laziness or negligence.
