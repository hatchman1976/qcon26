# Slide 06 — Dynamic economic envelope

## Slide copy

Economic envelope

**AI can make each unit cheaper while making the system more expensive.**

- **Point:** metered use, infrastructure consumption, committed work
- **Outer boundary:** non-viability, shaped by financial capacity and cost
- **Inner guardrail:** budgets, quotas, and ROI targets

Workflow cost = compute + platform + assurance + recovery

**Unit economics are not aggregate economics.**

## Speaker notes

Distinguish three different movements in the animation.

Increased usage, token spend, and committed work move the operating point toward the economic boundary. A genuine change in sustainable financial capacity—revenue, funding, runway, financing cost, or the cost base required to operate—can expand or contract the actual economic envelope. Budgets, quotas, and ROI hurdles are management controls inside that envelope: show them as a perceived or policy guardrail, not automatically as the functional boundary.

Do not animate the same event as all three. A large token bill normally moves the point. A funding shock may contract the boundary. A cost-cutting target may move the inner guardrail.

The token bill is only one term. The relevant denominator is not cost per generated token or artifact; it is cost per completed, verified, valuable outcome. Include model use, reserved compute or licenses, tools and data, integration, evaluation, human verification, coordination, rework, incident recovery, and abandoned output.

The economics differ by layer. Model laboratories face training, serving, talent, and compute commitments. Hyperscalers face data-centre capital, power, depreciation, utilization, and hardware replacement. Adopters face seats, tokens, tools, integration, evaluation, review, rework, and recovery.

Use “absorbing extraordinary amounts of cash,” not “every company is burning cash.” Capital expenditure creates assets and is not the same as an accounting loss. Some firms remain highly profitable while making very large commitments; others have negative free cash flow after investment.

The unit-versus-aggregate paradox is visible in Alphabet's own disclosures: it reported a 78% reduction in Gemini serving unit costs during 2025 while guiding 2026 capital expenditure to $175–185 billion, up from $91.4 billion in 2025. Lower unit costs can induce enough additional demand and capacity investment to raise the total bill.

Amazon reported trailing-twelve-month free cash flow of negative $7.6 billion in Q2 2026, primarily attributing the year-over-year reduction to higher property-and-equipment purchases that primarily reflected AI investment. Oracle reported fiscal-2026 capital expenditure of $55.7 billion and free cash flow of negative $23.7 billion as it invested in cloud infrastructure. Microsoft and Meta remained profitable, but their disclosures show how close quarterly operating cash generation can come to being absorbed by infrastructure investment.

Economic control is not inherently unsafe. Rate limits, admission control, model routing, caching, batching, smaller contexts, and stopping low-value workflows can restore margin. The risk transfer occurs when the organization protects AI output or growth by cutting people, review, testing, maintenance, or recovery capacity. That may improve the visible economic metric while contracting the workload envelope and consuming performance margin.

Financial measurement, management decisions, and throttling mechanisms create the response. The boundary itself does not repel the point.
